The Car Wash Invariant

When “I Want to Walk” Defeats “The Car Is What Needs Washing”

Written by Zachary Zhang • Dimension of Thought Archives

The setup is almost insultingly small: I am at home. My car needs washing. The car wash is thirty feet away. I desperately want to stretch my legs. Should I drive there or walk there?

A surprising number of language models answer: walk. Thirty feet is nothing. Walking is healthy. Starting a car for such a tiny distance feels ridiculous. Every sentence sounds locally sensible — right up until the cashier asks the one question the model forgot to preserve: Where is the car?

The payload is the vehicle

The problem is not really transportation advice. It is an object-role problem. The goal is not “get Zach to the car wash.” The goal is “get the car washed.” The required object is the car. The car begins at home. Therefore the car has to arrive at the wash.

The payload is the vehicle.

Once that invariant is explicit, the puzzle collapses. Drive the car thirty feet. Park it where the wash needs it. Then, if the legs are still filing a formal complaint after a day of sitting, go take an actual walk.

Why smart models still miss it

The interesting failure is that the model often possesses every relevant fact. It knows the car needs washing. It knows the wash needs the car. It may even say both of those things in the same answer. But the prompt contains several highly salient cues — “thirty feet,” “stretch my legs,” “sitting all day,” “walk or drive” — and those cues activate a familiar advice pattern: tiny distance plus desire for exercise means walk.

The model then solves the wrong latent task beautifully. It optimizes my personal movement while dropping the object that gives the trip its purpose. In some versions it notices the contradiction halfway through, but rather than replacing the original hypothesis, it patches around it: walk over first, inspect the wash, walk back, then drive. The reasoning has improved, yet it is still paying rent to the first bad frame.

A miniature ARC-style failure

This is why the prompt became one of my favorite informal model tests. It has the same flavor as harder abstract-reasoning tasks: the surface contains many plausible patterns, but only one transformation or invariant actually governs the answer. The challenge is not encyclopedic knowledge. It is identifying what must remain true.

A model can be astonishing at code, mathematics, prose, or tool use and still get caught by a five-second object-binding problem. That contrast is exactly what the Trollability Suite is interested in. Intelligence is not one smooth scalar. Sometimes the giant reasoning engine trips over a thirty-foot driveway.

Failure signature

Salience capture: vivid preference cues dominate the causal goal. Role-binding drift: the traveler becomes the central object instead of the car. Local coherence over global coherence: each sentence sounds reasonable while the plan as a whole cannot accomplish the task.

The best answers do not merely remember more facts. They choose the right invariant early enough that the rest of the answer has nowhere absurd to go.
